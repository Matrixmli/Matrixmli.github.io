Discovering Semi-Private Social Networks: Structural Bias from Private–Private Edge Censoring

Dmitry Zinoviev (Suffolk University) and Michael Li (Wayland High School)
Presented at NetSci 2026, the International Conference on Network Science · Boston, June 2026

The question

On social media, many users hide their friend lists. A private user can still be seen through their public friends, but a connection between two private users disappears entirely. How does that hidden pattern distort what researchers measure about a network?

What we did

We studied a Facebook network of about 12,000 Russian-, Ukrainian-, and Belarusian-speaking users in New England (about 103,000 connections), where roughly 40% of users keep their friend lists private. We simulated two ways of losing connections, removing the same number each time and repeating each experiment 30 times:

  • Privacy-driven: mark some users private and hide only the connections between private users
  • Random (baseline): remove connections at random

What we found

  • The two mechanisms distort the network in very different ways, even when the same amount of data is missing.
  • At a realistic 40% privacy level, the visible network likely underestimates how dense and tightly clustered the real network is (by roughly 20% and 18%).
  • Who is private matters: when private users are mostly the less-connected ones, as in the real data, the biggest distortion is undercounting communities (by about 25%).
  • Reconstructing hidden networks works better when models account for how privacy actually works, not just how much data is missing.
